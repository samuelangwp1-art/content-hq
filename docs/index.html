installed-before-approval

LinkedIn post
I sell signage for a living, and 1 client paid for the same sign twice because of a single question.

"Can we just install it and sort the approval after?"

The sign went up on opening day, before ABCi approval had landed.

The timeline was never planned properly. They wanted it rushed, approval could not come through in that window, and opening day does not wait for a government queue.

Then the comment came back, about the Jawi proportion. Nothing dramatic. That kind of comment comes back on plenty of submissions and costs nothing when it lands on a drawing.

This one landed on a sign already bolted to the wall.

That is the whole difference. A comment on paper is free and normal. The same comment on an installed sign means unbolting it, reworking it, and putting it back up. They paid for that.

Nothing was wrong with the design. The fix was 1 line item, not a rebuild. The problem sat earlier than the sign shop. The opening date got fixed before the approval did.

Not the designer's mistake, and not ABCi being difficult. A sign installed one step too early.

If you have an opening date locked in, ask your vendor for the ABCi approval date before you ask for the install date. That is the one that decides whether you open on time.

I sell signage for a living, and there is 1 question that decides whether your vendor can even file your application.

A shop owner cannot submit their own signage application in Brunei. Only an advertising company registered with ABCi can do it.

I still meet people who find this out after they have picked a design, sometimes after they have already paid a deposit to a fabricator. By then it is too late to plan around.

The application has to go through a registered company. Not the shop owner, and not just anyone who owns a cutting machine and can print you a nice looking sign.

It changes who you need in the room and when. If the vendor quoting your job is not ABCi-registered, they cannot lodge the application, no matter how good the design looks. You would need a second party just for that step. That adds time you did not budget for.

Much easier to have that conversation before any money moves, not after a deposit is sitting with the wrong company.

So ask the specific question instead. Are you ABCi-registered.

Send that 1 message before you pay any deposit. It tells you whether the company in front of you can take your job all the way to a sign on the wall.

I sell signage for a living, and 7 things have to be settled before ABCi ever sees your application.

"I just need a sign made, how hard can it be?"

Before an ABCi submission can even go in, 7 things have to be settled. Not roughly decided. Settled.

Site plan. Where exactly it fixes on the building. The proposed wording. Lettering size. Materials, mounting, colour and height. The proposed Jawi wording. Your logo, plus its written definition.

Most of that list is quick. The site plan and fixture point get sorted in one site visit. Materials, mounting, colour and height is a conversation you are already having.

The 2 that catch people out are the Jawi wording and the logo definition.

Nobody walks in already knowing either answer. Jawi wording needs translating properly, not run through an app. A logo needs a written definition of what it is, not just the file on your invoices.

Those 2 sit outside what a shop owner has usually thought about. They stall a submission longest.

If you are starting a signage job, write those 2 down first. Get the Jawi translation moving and the logo definition written out before you even call a vendor.

Ask which of the 7 plans your vendor has already started, before you pay a deposit.

I quote signage for a living, and every job runs on 3 separate clocks that clients only ever hear as one.

"So how long does the whole thing take?"

Jawi translation is roughly a week. ABCi approval is 2 to 3 weeks. Fabrication is 1 to 3 weeks. Three different people, three different speeds.

I am not going to add them up for you. The moment you treat them as one number you have already misjudged the job.

A client can watch fabrication. Material arrives, gets cut, gets assembled. So that is the queue they budget time for.

Translation sits with a translator. Approval sits with ABCi. Neither has anything to look at, so in most people's heads they take zero days.

The queue that quietly decides your opening date is usually the one nobody was watching.

None of the 3 is difficult on its own. The problem is planning around one queue and assuming the other two happen on their own.

Start the Jawi wording early, because nothing can be submitted until it is right.

Then ask for the Jawi wording date before you agree any opening date. It is the only queue you can begin early, and it is the one people start last.

I sell signage for a living, and 1 layout decision saves about a week of rework on every shopfront.

"Why is the Jawi bigger than my own shop name?"

On a Brunei shopfront the size order is fixed. Jawi biggest, Malay next, English smallest. I tell them it is an ABCi requirement. Nobody has ever argued.

But most people hear that as a rule to comply with. I treat it as the first decision in the layout, and that difference is worth about a week of rework.

Watch what happens if you design English first. You pick the fonts, get the spacing right, then try to fit Jawi in afterward. It breaks. You are placing your biggest piece last, into a layout never built to hold it. The spacing cramps, or the English shrinks past what the client wanted, or the board goes back to plan stage.

Design the Jawi first and none of that happens. It sets the scale. Malay and English fill the space around it instead of fighting it for room. Same reason you do not add a headline after the body text is already laid out.

The rule is not the interesting part. It tells you what order to work in, and almost nobody uses it that way.

Ask your designer to place the Jawi first, before any English is set. Then check which language got placed first in your current draft.

I have quoted builds for 4 years, and the gap between two quotes comes down to the same 4 things.

"Why is his price half of yours?"

I used to answer that with a lecture about quality. Never worked once. The honest answer is that the two numbers are not answers to the same question.

Cheaper material. Slower service, because a smaller shop takes longer to reply. Longer lead time, because the cheap job sits behind the ones that paid full. And print quality, which catches people, because sticker printing and PVC printing are not one product sold at two prices.

None of that makes the cheaper vendor dishonest. One quote is pricing a build that has to survive the season. The other is pricing a build that has to look right on delivery day. Sometimes that is genuinely all you need.

Here is the real problem. I know which is which by reading the spec. You do not. So the total is the only thing you can see, and you choose on the one number that tells you least.

So stop comparing totals. Ask both vendors to add 2 lines to the quote. The material type, written in words. The lead time, written in days. On the quote, not over the phone.

Send that request to both vendors this week, before you sign anything.

I have quoted builds for 4 years, and a reference photo reliably tells me only 1 thing about the job.

A client sends a photo and says "make it like this one."

It never once means what the sender assumed.

Same shape, usually. But there is a different material behind it, a different finish on the surface, and a different structure holding it up. None of that is visible on a phone screen.

The photo is not a spec. It is a feeling someone had for half a second while scrolling.

There is more information in the frame than people use. 4 things worth looking at.

What is it standing on, and is that base visible or tucked out of shot. Where is the light coming from, because a lot of what reads as premium is just strong lighting on ordinary material. What is it actually made of, as opposed to what it has been finished to resemble. And how was it shot, because a wide lens held low makes anything look bigger than it is.

None of that is written into the photo. All of it has to get built.

Write one line under it saying what you like. The finish. The weight. The way the light sits on it.

Ask yourself what you actually like about the photo, and send that line with it.

logo-doesnt-fabricate
9.99 10 cards
LinkedIn post
I have quoted signage for 4 years, and 3 things exist on a wall that simply do not exist on your screen.

"But the logo already looks perfect."

Four years quoting fabrication and I still see this on nearly every job.

The hairline stroke that reads fine on a laptop has no thickness once you cut it from metal or acrylic. It either gets fatter or it snaps off the router bed.

The tight counter in a lowercase a or e closes up the moment you shrink it to real size, or fills in completely once there is a light source behind it.

Drop shadows and gradients do not exist in a physical material. You can fake a gradient with layered vinyl and it never matches. Colour drifts anyway once you match a Pantone across acrylic, paint and LED.

None of this means the logo is bad. It was drawn for a screen, which has no thickness, no weight, and no light behind it. A sign has all 3, whether the person who drew it thought about that or not.

The fix is simple and most designers hand it over without complaint.

Ask your designer for a single colour, single thickness version before you sign off on anything. Strip the gradient, strip the shadow, pick one line weight throughout.

Send that flat version to your fabricator and ask them to quote against it.

I have watched crowds walk past hundreds of booths. You get about 2 seconds, from an angle, from someone still moving.

Not head on. Not standing still. The booths that stop people almost never win on budget.

The most common mistake is treating the back wall like a poster. Company name, tagline, 5 bullet points, logo soup along the bottom. Nobody walking an aisle reads a paragraph. If one idea survives a glance from across the aisle, that idea is doing the work.

Eye level and hip level do different jobs, and most booths use them the same way. What is up high pulls someone in from the aisle. What is at hip level is for the person who already stopped and is close enough to read detail or pick something up. Put the same message at both and you have wasted the second one.

The other thing that kills booths is a counter used as a wall. It looks tidy from the exhibitor's side and reads as "we are not open to you" from the aisle. An open corner does more to pull people in than any banner above it.

Stand where the aisle actually approaches your booth, walk it at the pace people walk, and time 2 seconds. Whatever you could not read in that window is what needs fixing first.

Ask where the opening is before you approve the layout.

A signmaker will build your spelling mistake exactly, at full size, in metal.

Wording on a sign is not copy. It is a manufactured part.

The wording gets typed once, usually into a message, and then it is copied forward into the drawing, the submission and the cutting file. Nobody types it again, so nobody reads it again. The mistake travels the whole way with the job.

A stray space, a missing dot, a shop name that was still being decided when the message was sent. All of it survives to install day, because everyone downstream assumes the person upstream checked.

A design revision costs you a few days. A wrong letter costs you the part.

Read the wording out loud, letter by letter, before you approve the drawing. That is the last point where it is still just text.

the-wall-decides-the-sign
0 cards
LinkedIn post
Nobody asks what the wall is made of. It is the first thing that decides what your sign can be.

The wall is not where the sign goes. It is part of the sign.

A glass shopfront, a plaster face, metal cladding and a tiled column are four different jobs. Each one takes a different fixing. Some of them cannot hold a sign at all without reaching through to the structure behind, and that changes the depth, the weight limit and sometimes the design itself.

What happens instead is the design gets approved first, and the wall gets looked at on install day. That is when someone finds out the front is glass and the mount has nowhere to go. The sign is already built by then, so the choice is a redesign or a compromise on site.

The compromise is the expensive one, because it is decided in an hour by whoever is standing there with a drill.

Ask what the sign will be fixed into, before you approve the design. Then send a photo of the bare surface, not the shopfront.
